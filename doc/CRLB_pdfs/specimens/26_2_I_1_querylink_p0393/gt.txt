This composition was painted by Polidoro and
Maturino on the façade of Palazzo Milesi, Rome,
in the mid-1520s (as discussed in the short intro-
duction above). It formed one section of the frieze
between the first and second floors. The subject
has been widely - but in my opinion wrongly -
identified as the family of Darius before Alexan-
der.2 Darius fled after the battle of Issus but his
family was captured. When Alexander visited
his prisoners' tents, Darius's mother mistook
Haephestion for the general because of his greater
height, giving him the obeisance due to a con-
queror (Arrian, Anabasis, II, 12; Plutarch, XXXIII,
21; Quintus Curtius Rufus, History of Alexander,
III, xi, 23-25; Diodorus Siculus, Bibliotheca historica,
XVII, 37; Valerius Maximus, Dicta et facta, IV, 7). If
this identification is correct it should be Darius's
mother, Sisygambis, who kneels before Alexan-
der and Haephestion, not a bearded man (who
anyhow has no place in this episode). Sisygambis
should be accompanied by Darius's wife, Stateira,
their son and two daughters, but Polidoro's com-
position shows a young woman accompanied by
an older couple who are petitioning the enthroned
commander.
The subject has also been identified as the Con-
tinence of Scipio (Livy, Ab urbe condita, XXVI, 50;
Petrarch, Africa, IV, 375-388, Polybius, Histories,
X, 19; Valerius Maximus, Dicta et facta, IV, iii, 1),3
which, to my mind, fits better. Scipio had cap-
tured the city of New Carthage (Carthago Nova)
in 209 B.C. and among his prizes was a beauti-
ful (but unnamed) woman. On learning that she
was to be married, Scipio reunited her with her
betrothed, Allucius (also sometimes called Indi-
bilis), and generously gave the ransom that her
parents had provided to the young couple as a
wedding gift. The woman at the far right must be
the bride, the kneeling man, her father, and the
old woman at her side, her mother. The parents
inform Scipio that their daughter is betrothed and
beg for his mercy, to which he accedes with a gra-
cious gesture of his right hand. Early depictions
of this subject tended not to show the bride reu-
nited with Allucius but as still in Scipio's custody,
and it was only later that it became usual to depict
the youthful couple together and giving thanks to
their benefactor.4
This composition can be compared to Giulio
Romano's treatment of the Continence of Scipio
(Fig. 225) in a circular format on the ceiling of the
Sala del Imperatore, also called the Sala di Cesare,
at Palazzo Te, Mantua, probably datable around
1531-32.5 This composition became well-known
through several early woodcuts and engravings.6
Giulio made very literal use of Polidoro's com-
position showing Scipio in an almost identical
pose, seen from the back and from below, with
his legs pulled round to the right and his right
arm extended.7 Neither Polidoro nor Giulio were
bothered that Scipio's face was hidden from the
spectator. A preliminary pen and ink drawing by
Giulio in the Ambrosiana, Milan,8 shows that in
his first draft the kneeling man - who has his arms
crossed on his chest - was also depicted exactly as
in Polidoro's design, but Giulio then re-drew his
right arm so as to raise it up in surprise. A more
finished modello in the Städelsches Kunstinstitut,
Frankfurt a. M.,9 confirms that Giulio made this
man much younger in appearance and intended
him to represent Allucius. But the major change
that he introduced was to extend the composition
